What missed service calls cost a dealership, and how to answer every one

Every unanswered call to the service department is a booking, an upsell or a returning customer that may go somewhere else. Here's how to put a number on it and fix it.

By AIfy team·October 10, 2026·4 min read

Key takeaways

  • Missed service calls cluster at predictable times: the morning rush, lunch, end of day and after hours.
  • You can estimate the cost with four numbers you already have: missed calls, the share that were bookings, how many never call back, and your average repair order.
  • Many "is my car ready?" calls can be answered automatically from the repair order, which frees advisors for the customers in front of them.
  • Start with after-hours calls: low risk, easy to measure, and no change to how the drive works during the day.

Ask any service manager when the phones are worst and you'll get the same answer: first thing in the morning, over lunch, and right before close. Those are exactly the times advisors are busiest on the drive. The result is predictable: calls ring out, go to a voicemail box nobody checks until later, or sit on hold until the customer gives up.

Why service calls get missed

It's rarely a people problem. It's a timing problem:

  • The morning rush. Advisors are writing up drop-offs while the phone rings with people asking for same-day appointments.
  • Lunch and breaks. Coverage thins out exactly when customers are on their own lunch break and finally have time to call.
  • Status calls. A large share of inbound calls are "is my car ready?", which take an advisor away from the drive to look up something the system already knows.
  • After hours. Many customers think about booking service in the evening. If nobody answers, some will book online, some will call back, and some will call the shop down the road.

Put a number on it

You don't need a consultant to estimate the cost. Pull these from your phone system and DMS for a typical month:

  1. Missed or abandoned calls to the service line (including after-hours calls).
  2. The share of those that were booking calls, as opposed to status checks or wrong numbers. If you don't know, listen to a sample of voicemails or recorded calls.
  3. The share that never called back. Compare missed numbers against booked appointments over the following week.
  4. Your average customer-pay repair order.

Then multiply: missed calls × share that were bookings × share that never called back × average repair order.

Here's a purely illustrative example, not data from any store. Say a store misses 300 service calls a month, a third were booking calls, half of those never called back, and the average repair order is $350. That's 300 × 1/3 × 1/2 × $350 = $17,500 a month in work that may have gone elsewhere, before counting the customer's future visits. Your numbers will differ; the point is to run them.

What "answering every call" can look like

An AI voice agent for the service department works like a well-trained receptionist who never takes a break:

  • Answers on the first ring, day or night, with your store's greeting.
  • Books appointments by your rules: which services, which times, which bays and advisors, how long each job takes, and when loaners are available.
  • Answers common questions about hours, prices, shuttles and loaners from information you approve.
  • Handles status calls by looking up the repair order and telling the customer whether the vehicle is ready and what's owed.
  • Checks open recalls and offers to fix them during the same visit.
  • Texts a confirmation and puts a short note in the advisor's queue.
  • Hands off to a person when the caller is upset, asks for someone, or needs a judgement call.

A rollout plan that doesn't disrupt the drive

  1. After-hours calls first. These are calls nobody is answering today, so there's nothing to lose and it's easy to measure what comes in.
  2. Overflow and lunch. Once your team trusts how it books and hands off, let it pick up when advisors are busy or after a set number of rings.
  3. Outbound follow-ups. Due-for-service reminders, declined-work follow-ups and open recalls, by call or text.

At each stage, review real call summaries with your service manager and adjust the rules.

What to check before you choose a solution

  • Integration: can it book directly into your scheduler, or will it capture requests for an advisor to confirm? Both can work; know which one you're getting.
  • Your rules: can you control services, times, capacity and what it won't discuss?
  • Hand-off: what happens when someone wants a person? A warm transfer with context beats a cold transfer.
  • Data: where are recordings and transcripts stored, and can you export them?
  • Measurement: you should see every call, its outcome and any booking it created.

Frequently asked questions

Will customers know they're talking to AI?

They should. A good setup says so up front, sounds natural, and makes it easy to reach a person. Customers mostly care about getting booked quickly and accurately.

Does it replace service advisors?

No. It takes the repetitive calls (booking, status, simple questions) so advisors can spend their time on the customers in the lane and on selling recommended work.

Can it book into our DMS or scheduler?

Often, depending on what your systems allow. Where a direct connection isn't possible, the agent captures the request with all the details and an advisor confirms it.

How quickly can a store go live?

An after-hours pilot is the quickest start, typically a matter of weeks. Timelines depend mainly on integrations.
